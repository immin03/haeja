This tiny snack got banned in North Korea. Not a weapon. Not a phone. A snack. And then ten thousand of them flew over the border. On balloons.

Hi! I'm Kachi, the little magpie who brings you the weirdest true stories from Korea. Today's star is Orion's Choco Pie. It costs less than a coffee, sits in every convenience store here, and somehow lived a wilder life than most people. Let's go!

Meet the Orion Choco Pie. Two soft cakes, a marshmallow middle, and a chocolate coat. Simple, right? Wait till you see where it's been.

Soldiers traded it like money. Russians fell in love with it over tea. Vietnamese families put it on their ancestors' altars. And at the very end, a soldier ran across the most guarded border on Earth, and asked for one. Stick around for that one.

It all starts in 1973, in a hotel in the American South. A Korean snack company researcher is on a business trip, and with his coffee, they bring him a little chocolate marshmallow cake. Its American cousin is the MoonPie. The story goes that a coal miner asked a baker for a snack as big as the moon, and the baker took him literally. One bite, and he's thinking: Korea needs this.

Back home, his team tinkers for a year, and in 1974, Choco Pie rolls off the line. Korea was still pretty poor back then. A chocolate cake? That was a little luxury.

Of course, a hit snack gets copied. In 1979, a big rival launched its own Choco Pie. Twenty years later, Orion took them to court over the name. And lost! The judge basically said: Choco Pie is so famous, it's just a normal word now. Like Band-Aid.

For forty-two years, there was only one Choco Pie. One flavor. That's it. Then in March 2016, Orion launched banana. A team of ten researchers had spent three years testing twenty flavors to pick it. Within three weeks, more than fourteen million packs were gone, and people were hunting store to store to find one. And it didn't stop there. In 2020, a spring strawberry blossom edition sold five million units in just three weeks. Koreans really do line up for a new Choco Pie.

So kids did something very Korean with it. They shared it. One cake, broken into four tiny pieces. There's a word for that feeling, and it's on the box: jeong.

Jeong is hard to translate. It's your grandma saying you look thin, and then feeding you four meals in one hour. It's love, but sticky. Like marshmallow.

In 1989, Orion built its TV ads around that feeling. No big sales pitch. Just tiny moments. A kid quietly sharing with a friend who's having a bad day. The idea was simple: you don't have to say it out loud. The cake says it for you. Ask Koreans of a certain age, and they can still hum the jingle.

Now, almost every young Korean man serves in the military for about a year and a half. Food is plain. Snacks are rare. So Choco Pie becomes money. For trainees, it's basically treasure.

And here's the joke every Korean guy knows. On Sundays, religious services on base hand out free snacks. So suddenly, a lot of soldiers become very, very spiritual. Church in the morning, temple after lunch, whoever has the better snacks.

At night, Choco Pies turn into poker chips. You borrow one, you owe one. Lose a bet, pay in pies.

And birthdays? There's no bakery on base. So soldiers stack Choco Pies into a tower, stick a candle on top, and sing. Ask a Korean guy who served. There's a good chance he's blown out a candle on a Choco Pie cake.

Quick quiz before we go on. How many Choco Pies do you think Russians eat in a year? A million? A hundred million? Guess in the comments, then keep watching. But one country was about to love it even more. Russia. In the early nineties, the Soviet Union had just collapsed and shelves were empty. Russian traders sailed to Busan and stuffed giant bags with anything they could sell back home. And they kept grabbing Choco Pie.

It was perfect for Russia. No fridge needed. It survives a week on a train across Siberia.

And with a glass of hot tea at the end of the day? Chef's kiss. Then, around 2000, a TV ad made it legendary. A little boy asks: Mom, where's the Choco Pie? Except he mumbles it so badly, a whole generation of Russians heard something like: go give the duck a bath.

People are still talking about that ad, twenty-five years later. Grown adults, finally decoding one sentence from their childhood.

Today Orion bakes Choco Pies inside Russia, on five production lines. And they run at over a hundred and forty percent of their normal capacity. Basically, the machines never rest. So they're adding a whole new factory building near Moscow, just to make more.

And Russians don't stop at the original. Orion makes twelve different Choco Pies just for Russia. Raspberry, cherry, even watermelon. In 2025, Russia alone made up about a third of all Choco Pie sales on the planet.

Next stop, Vietnam, where Choco Pie got a promotion. During the Lunar New Year, families set up altars for their ancestors. Fruit, flowers, incense, and in a lot of homes, a box of Choco Pie. It's the snack you bring when you visit someone's home for the holiday. Showing up empty-handed? Not an option. By 2018, people in Vietnam were buying around six hundred million packs a year. Here, jeong became tình. Affection.

In China, Orion goes by Haoliyou, which means good friend. Pretty great name for a snack company. And jeong became rén, the old Confucian word for kindness. Same snack, three countries, three words for love. Fun fact: China is now Orion's biggest market. Bigger than Korea.

Okay. Now the strange part. Just north of the border was the Kaesong Industrial Complex, where South Korean companies ran factories with North Korean workers. Companies couldn't hand out extra cash. So the bonus was snacks. For many workers, it was the first time they'd ever tasted anything like it. And their kids at home? Even more excited. Between 2008 and 2014, one estimate says around 1.2 million boxes of Choco Pies went into North Korea this way. And the snack everyone wanted? You guessed it.

A lot of workers didn't eat them. They sold them. In 2010, a South Korean newspaper reported a single Choco Pie could go for nine dollars and fifty cents on the black market. A snack that costs pennies in Seoul.

For a government that controls almost everything people see and hear, that was a problem. A sweet little taste of the South, passed from hand to hand. In 2013 the complex shut down for five months. When it reopened, the Choco Pie bonus was off the table, and by 2014, reports said: no more Choco Pies. Hand out sausages, instant noodles and coffee instead.

And remember the balloons? That same year, activists in the South tied ten thousand Choco Pies to giant balloons and let them float north. A snack. Flying over the most guarded border in the world. You can't make this up.

Which brings us to my favorite part. In November 2017, a young North Korean soldier made a desperate run across the Joint Security Area, the one spot where soldiers from both sides stand face to face. He was badly hurt. Doctors in the South saved his life. And when he woke up, one of the first things he reportedly wanted was a Choco Pie. Orion heard, sent a hundred boxes to the hospital, and promised him Choco Pies for life. Small problem: for a while, doctors only let him have liquids. So the Choco Pies had to wait. Which, honestly, might be the hardest part of the whole story.

More than twelve billion Choco Pies have been sold around the world. Line them up, and they'd stretch past the moon and back. Which feels right, for a snack whose cousin was named after the moon.

So let's add it up. Inspired in Georgia. Named after love. Money in the army. Tea time in Russia. Offered to ancestors in Vietnam. Banned in the North. Today, about eighty-six percent of Choco Pie sales happen outside Korea.
